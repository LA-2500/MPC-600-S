Chapter 1Introduction

Welcome

The MPC 600 S is a pad-based sampler and sequencer for the Akai MPC Sample, built around sequences, tracks, programs and sounds. It has S600 programs based on Akai’s S-series samplers (two-oscillator synths, and S600 MULTI multisample instruments with an Auto Sampler that records them note by note), an arpeggiator with step patterns, warping and timestretching, effects for the input, the tracks and the master output, stem separation, and a sampler that records at low sample rates and bit depths.

It runs from the SD card. Nothing is flashed to the MPC Sample, and the original Akai software is left untouched: remove the card and the MPC Sample starts as it came.

Installing and Starting

The MPC 600 S comes as a zip named with its version (for example MPC-600-S-OS-v2.0J.zip; mpc-os.zip when built from the source), an autorun package for the MPC Sample’s SD card:

  1. Unzip it. Copy the autorun folder inside to the top level of an SD card (FAT32 or exFAT), its name and files unchanged.
  2. With the MPC Sample off, put the card in and turn it on. The MPC Sample starts as usual; a few seconds later the MPC 600 S takes over the screen. The title screen shows the version, then the main sequence page.
  3. To go back to the stock MPC Sample, turn it off and take the card out. To update, replace the autorun folder with the new one.

The separate Quick Start guide has these steps in full, with what to check if it does not start.

The OS keeps everything it saves in a folder named MPC600S at the top of the card, outside the autorun package, so updating the OS never touches your work:

Knobs and the Slider Catch Up

The knobs and slider are absolute: each has a position. When you turn one, it does not change the value until it reaches the value’s own position; from there it takes over. Nothing jumps when you first touch a control. This is Knob Mode CATCH, the way it starts; Additional Settings (Chapter 16) also has JUMP, where the value goes straight to the knob, and SCALE, where the value moves from where it is, in step with the knob, so the two meet at the ends of its travel.

Zoomed in on a waveform (hold SAMPLE and press B1 or B2), the Zone and Loop point knobs change behaviour: they move the point from where it is, in fine steps of 1/127 of what is on screen. The zoomed view follows the point you moved last (Start or End, Loop St or Loop End) and keeps it in the middle of the screen, even at the very beginning or end of the sound, where the view runs on past it.

Page Buttons Count a Whole Press

The buttons that open pages (KNOB FX, PAD FX, MUTE, LEVELS, LOOP, CHOP, SAMPLE SELECT, SEQ, SAMPLE REC and PAD BANK) act when the button comes back up, and only after a whole press: down, then up, held between 12 ms and 4 seconds. The panel can send a button message nobody pressed; a lone one is ignored, so pages do not open by themselves. Anywhere, a release with no press before it is ignored, and a tap of ERASE shorter than 12 ms (quicker than a finger) does not open the Erase page. Buttons that are held or keep time (SHIFT, SAMPLE, ERASE held with pads, NOTE REPEAT, B1–B3, −/+ and the transport) still act the moment they are pressed.

Pressing a Page’s Button Again

On every page, pressing the button that opened it again puts the cursor back on the page’s first field (on a few pages, on the field you will want most, such as Effect # on Input Effects).

Help

Press SHIFT + SAMPLE REC on any page to see that page’s help, and SHIFT + SEQ REC to see its second page. Where there are more, B1 <Prev> and B3 <Next> turn the pages and B2 <Exit> goes back. The help tells you what the page does, the keys you cannot see (the SHIFT combinations), and tips. A small helper stands at the side of the help pages, tapping her foot and pointing at the text with her stick.

The main sequence page has no help: there, the same two buttons move the play position back and forward a bar.

Undo and Redo

SHIFT + − undoes and SHIFT + + redoes, up to two steps. Undo covers audio commands (Discard, Convert, Print Effects, Warp and the like) and changes to the sequences (recording, erasing, editing bars). It does not undo settings such as a pad’s volume.

Definitions

  • Sound: a recording in memory: a sample.
  • Pad: one of 128 places in a program (16 pads in each of 8 banks), each with a sound and that sound’s settings.
  • Program: a whole set of 128 pads. There are 24. A program is MPC (drums) or S600 (a playable synth instrument, Chapter 9).
  • Memory: up to 64 sounds kept without being on a pad. A sound that leaves its last pad goes here instead of being lost.
  • Sequence: a piece of music up to 999 bars long. There are 99.
  • Track: one part of a sequence. Each sequence has 99 tracks, DRUM (plays a program) or MIDI (sends notes out).
  • Song: a list of sequences played in order. There are 20.

Playing the Pads

Pads play the active track’s program, through that track’s mixer channel. Full Level (SHIFT + Pad 1) plays every hit at full velocity.

16 Levels

LEVELS lays one sound across the 16 pads: Velocity (pad 1 softest to pad 16 hardest), then Tune (a chromatic scale, the root pad playing the sound’s own pitch), then off. SHIFT + LEVELS opens its page, where you choose the type, the sound and the root (A08 by default). In Tune, PAD BANK reaches further up and down: 128 tunings in all.

Chapter 3Recording Sequences

The Main Page

SEQ opens the main page: Play/Record at the top (sequence, tempo, bars, time signature, loop) and Track Data under it (track, type, on/off, MIDI channel, velocity %, program). The Now: line shows the position as bar.beat.tick.

The knobs are labelled at the bottom: K1 the track, K2 the tempo (whole BPM), K3 the track’s program. The slider steps through the tracks. B1 turns the track on or off; B2 and B3 step to the track before and after. SHIFT + B3 opens Copy Sequence (Chapter 6). Hold SHIFT and the key labels change to show these: B2 reads <Rename>, B3 <Copy>.

Renaming

Put the cursor on Seq, Trk or Pgm and press SHIFT + B2 to rename the sequence, the track or the track’s program. The wheel sets the highlighted letter, − and + move along the name (up to 16 letters), ERASE takes out a letter and SHIFT + ERASE the whole name, B3 <OK> keeps it and B1 <Cancel> leaves the old name. A MIDI track’s Pgm is a program number, so it has no name to change.

{{shot:seq_main_knobs|The main page}}

Recording

  1. Choose the sequence and the track to record on, on the main page.
  2. Press SEQ REC to arm a recording mode (see below). The light flashes while armed, and the display shows the mode.
  3. Press PLAY. If Count-in is on, the count-in comes first. (Pressed again while only playing, PLAY starts the sequence over from the start; the notes sounding stop first.)
  4. Play the pads, or a MIDI keyboard. Each note goes onto the active track, placed by Timing Correct (Chapter 4), with its velocity and how long you held it.
  5. Press STOP. SHIFT + − undoes the whole recording.

The Recording Modes

Pressed while stopped, SEQ REC steps through three settings: OVERDUB, then REC, then off. OVERDUB comes first because it never erases anything.

Punching In and Out

While the sequence is playing, press SEQ REC to punch in: recording starts there and then, in OVERDUB, without stopping or erasing anything. Press it again to punch out: the sequence keeps playing and recording stops. Undo covers a punch-in too.

Looping, Erasing and Holding Notes

  • Loop on the main page sends the sequence back to a bar at the end, so you can keep recording pass after pass.
  • Hold ERASE and hold pads while recording or playing: those pads’ notes are erased as the sequence passes them. Let go to stop erasing.
  • Hold NOTE REPEAT while recording: the repeats or the arpeggio are recorded as notes (Chapter 5).
  • A note’s length is how long you held the pad. With a sustain pedal set to it on MIDI Settings, the pedal holds notes on.

Recording Knobs and the Slider

While recording, moves of the knobs and slider on Volume, Pan, Cutoff, Resonance, Semi and Fine are recorded as well, on drum sounds and on S600 programs. They play back with the sequence, so a filter sweep or a pitch drop becomes part of the part. Holding ERASE with a pad also erases that pad’s recorded moves as the sequence passes them.

Tempo, Bars and Length

  • Tap Tempo: tap the button in time. SHIFT + TAP TEMPO opens the metronome page (click on/off, in play, volume, rate, count-in bars and type).
  • Bars: changing Bars on the main page asks first when it matters. Adding bars to a sequence with notes asks Duplicate sequence data?: Yes repeats the existing bars from bar 1 into the new ones, No leaves them empty. Taking bars away when the removed bars have notes asks Delete bars x-y? first.
  • Double and half the sequence: SHIFT + Pad 3 and SHIFT + Pad 2 do the same with one press.
  • Double and half speed: SHIFT + Pad 7 and SHIFT + Pad 6 double or halve the BPM, asking Apply note-value compensation?. No changes only the BPM, so the music plays faster or slower. Yes keeps it playing at the same speed: doubling moves every note twice as far along and doubles the bars; halving squeezes the music into the first half and repeats it in the second.

Audio Tracks

The MPC 600 S records audio onto a pad in time with the sequence, so a vocal, a guitar or a synth part plays back with the music as an audio track. The take is an ordinary sound on a pad, started by a note at the beginning of a track, so it can be edited, effected, warped and mixed like any other.

Recording an Audio Track

  1. Press SAMPLE REC twice for the Audiotrack Record page.
  2. Set the Input (LINE or MIC), the Mode (stereo or one of the mono choices), Count In (2 bars by default, or 1) and Length.
  3. Select the track the take should go on, on the main page beforehand (a DRUM track).
  4. Press <Rec>. The sequence plays from its start after the count-in, and the take begins exactly on the first beat of bar 1.
  5. When it ends (or you press STOP or <Stop>), press an empty pad. It gets the take, and a note at the very start of the active track, as long as the take, starts it whenever the sequence plays from the top.

How It Stays in Time

  • The take is lined up with the sequence’s first tick, not with the moment you pressed <Rec>: the count-in is cut off.
  • The time the sound takes to get through the MPC Sample’s input is measured and taken out, so a part you played along to the click lands where you played it.
  • The pad needs to be empty: the take never overwrites a sound. A full pad asks you to choose again.
  • On a MIDI track the take still goes on the pad, but no note is added: select a DRUM track to have it play with the sequence.

Live Looper

<Live Loop> on Audiotrack Record opens a page for takes that drop in while the sequence keeps playing, one pass at a time, as a looper does. Each take goes on its own pad with a note at the start of the active track, so the layers build up without the sequence stopping.

  1. On the main page, set the sequence’s Loop to TO BAR 1 and select a DRUM track.
  2. On Live Looper, select an empty pad: press it, or turn the Pad field. The take goes there; a pad with a sound is refused.
  3. Press <Rec>. Stopped, the sequence counts in (Count In) and the first pass is the take. Playing, the take starts on the next bar 1.
  4. At the end of the pass the sequence carries on, and the take drops onto the pad straight away (how it is converted: Live Loop Conversion, below), faded in over 5 ms from where the new pass is. From the next pass it plays from the top with the sequence.
  5. Select the next empty pad and press <Rec> again for another layer. Nothing has to stop.
  6. Not happy with a take? <Del Take> (B2) deletes the last one: its sound leaves the pad, its note leaves the track, and the pad is selected again, so <Rec> records over it on the next pass. Pressed again, it deletes the take before, back through the last 16. It is one step of Undo as well.

Live Loop Conversion

A take is recorded at 44.1 kHz and 16 bits. Turning it into a sound at the page’s settings is the same work as on Sample New Sound: for each new sample, a windowed-sinc filter (the anti-aliasing, and the pitch-up) reads the take between its samples; then the take is normalized to its peak and rounded to its bits. The filter is nearly all of the work, and normalizing needs the whole take, since its loudest point can come last. Loop Conv on Additional Settings (Chapter 16) chooses when that work is done:

Loop ConvHow it worksWhat you hear
IMMEDIATE (default)The filter runs while the pass is recorded, a background thread following a few milliseconds behind the input. When the pass ends, only its last few milliseconds, the normalize and the bits are left: well under a millisecond’s work for 10 seconds of stereo on a computer, a few milliseconds on the MPC Sample.The take at its own rate, bits and pitch from the start of the next pass, a few milliseconds in (the input’s delay and that work), faded in.
AFTER 1ST LOOPThe take drops in as recorded, 44.1 kHz and 16 bits with no pitch-up, as soon as the pass ends. It is converted in the background while it plays, and the converted sound takes the pad’s place for the passes after.The first pass after the take clean and full-range, then the converted sound. Nothing runs while you record, so it suits longer takes: the processor is free for the pass, and the conversion has a whole loop to finish in.

The result is the same either way, sample for sample: only the moment it is made differs. The tests check that the converted take is identical whichever way it is made.

Working With Audio Tracks

  • Record several takes onto different pads, and mute or solo them on Track Mute (PAD MUTE) to pick the best.
  • Put each take on a separate track to give it a mixer channel, EQ and effect sends.
  • Use Warp (Chapter 13) to fit a take to a new tempo, and Print Effects to commit an effect to it.
  • If the sequence plays from the middle, the take does not start: it is started by its note at the beginning, as any one-shot sound would be.

Chapter 4Timing Correct and the Swing Algorithms

Timing Correct

SHIFT + Pad 14 opens the Timing Correct page; SHIFT + Pad 10 turns it off and back on. As you record, each note moves to the nearest step of the Note value: 1/4, 1/4T, 1/8, 1/8T, 1/16, 1/16T, 1/32, 1/32T, 1/64, 1/64T, or OFF. Shift timing then moves every note a few ticks earlier or later. Move Existing applies all of this to notes already recorded, over a range of bars, for all notes or the pads you press.

The knobs: K1 Swing (in SP12 mode, the SP12’s six settings), K2 Shift amount, K3 the Move Existing track. Each takes the cursor to its field, as Knob Mode sets.

{{shot:timing_correct|The Timing Correct page}}

How Swing Works

Swing works on pairs of steps. With a 1/16 note value, each pair of 1/16 notes makes an 1/8 note; the first note of the pair stays where it is and the second is moved later. The Swing % says how far through the pair the second note lands: 50% is straight (half-way), 66% is a triplet feel (two thirds of the way), and 75% is the most.

Swing applies to the straight note values 1/8, 1/16 and 1/32. The triplet values are never swung. It applies to notes as they are recorded, to Move Existing, and to Note Repeat and the arpeggiator as they play.

The sequencer counts 96 ticks per quarter note (96 PPQN). A 1/16 note is 24 ticks, an 1/8 is 48, a 1/32 is 12. At 90 BPM one tick lasts about 6.9 milliseconds.

The MPC 600 S has four swing timings, because the way a Swing % becomes a delay differs from machine to machine, and the difference is audible. Sequencer Res on Additional Settings (Chapter 16) chooses which: MPC 3000, MPC60, MPC 2000 or SP12.

MPC 3000: Fixed Steps

The MPC 3000 setting moves in fixed steps: the swung 1/32 note moves a whole number of ticks, from 0 to 6, and the delay doubles for each larger note value.

1/32 delay = (Swing − 50) × 12 ÷ 50, rounded to the nearest whole tick (0 to 6)
1/16 delay = 2 × the 1/32 delay    1/8 delay = 4 × the 1/32 delay

So at 1/16 every setting lands on an even number of ticks, and 66% gives exactly 8 ticks: a perfect 2:1 triplet. Settings in between snap to the nearest step: 52% is still straight at 1/16, for example.

MPC60: As Measured

The MPC60 setting places notes where grooves recorded from a real MPC60 put them, at every setting from 50 to 75. At 1/16 it moves one tick at a time, where the MPC 3000 moves two, so the two part by a tick at 11 of the 26 settings. Its one surprise is 64, which lands on 6 ticks, the same as 63, where an even progression would give 7. It was measured that way, and it plays that way.

Chapter 5Note Repeat and the Arpeggiator

Note Repeat

Hold NOTE REPEAT and hold pads: they repeat at the Rate (1/4 to 1/32T), swung at 1/8, 1/16 and 1/32. Press harder for louder. While NOTE REPEAT is held, B1–B3 switch to another rate (the page shows which); B Keys sets whether they work while held (HOLD) or stay on until pressed again (TOGGLE). SHIFT + NOTE REPEAT opens the page; B2 <Rate> steps the rate.

The Arpeggiator

Set Type to ARPEGGIATOR and the held pads play one at a time in a pattern instead. Hold NOTE REPEAT and the pads as before.

The knobs work from where they are, a step for each small turn: K1 the Rate, K2 the Category, K3 the Pattern. B2 <Rate> still steps the rate, and B3 <Edit> opens Pattern Edit.

Kinds of Pattern

  • Chord patterns (Standard, Variation) go through the held pads, lowest first: Up, Down, Up/Down, Down/Up, Up/Down Incl, As Played, Random, Chord, and variations such as Thumb, Pinky, Converge, Up + Octave, Stutter and 3-Step.

Step Patterns

A step pattern is up to 16 steps, each played at the Rate from the lowest pad held. Each step is a note, an octave, an accent, a slide, a tie or a rest: enough for basslines, sequenced leads and the squelching, sliding lines of acid house.

AccentThe step at full velocity (the pad’s pressure). Steps without an accent play at 70% of it, so accents stand out.

Program Config

SHIFT + SAMPLE opens Program Config: the program’s pads and their sounds, a bank at a time. Type is MPC (drums), S600 (each pad a two-oscillator synth, Chapter 9) or S600 MULTI (a multisample instrument, Chapter 10). Snd: gives the selected pad any sound in memory; with SHIFT, the wheel steps only through the sounds in memory that no pad plays (No unused sounds in memory if there are none).

Copy and Swap with another program. After B1 <Copy> or B2 <Swap>, the wheel picks the program whose pad it goes to: this one to start (the prompt reads A02 to (wheel: Pgm)), or any of the 24 (A02 to Pgm 02 NAME). Then press the pad (any bank). Copy puts the pad, its sound and its settings onto that program’s pad (Copied A02 to P02 A05); Swap trades the two pads. Undo puts them back.

A sound a pad lets go of stays in memory unless another pad plays it, and a sound loaded or recorded over a pad keeps the old one in memory too, so nothing leaves memory without being deleted. Memory holds up to 512 sounds.

A pad remembers how it played a sound it lets go of: its start, end and loop, and its name. Put that sound back on that pad (the Sample: field, Snd: in Program Config, or a load) and the pad plays it as before, so a zone (Break-03) that had another sound put on it by mistake comes back as the zone, not the whole sound. On another pad, a sound starts whole. This is kept until power-off.

Chapter 9S600 Programs

What an S600 Program Is

An S600 program plays its pads as instruments rather than as drums: each pad is a two-oscillator synth, its own sound the first oscillator and Layer 2 (another pad’s sound) the second, both through the same envelopes, filter and LFO. (For an instrument built from many samples across the keyboard, see S600 MULTI, Chapter 10.) Set a program’s Type to S600 on Program Config and every pad in it gets a second set of settings, its S600 settings, kept apart from its drum settings. The pad’s sound, trim, loop, volume and tuning are shared; everything else is the S600’s own. Switch the program back to MPC and its drum settings are exactly as you left them.

On an S600 program, SAMPLE opens the S600 pages instead of the drum pages, and MIDI keys play the Instrument (Inst on Program Config: A01 to start, or LAST PAD for the pad pressed last) chromatically from its Root. 16 Levels Tune plays a sound across the pads.

Root Note and Root Auto-Detect

Root Note (on the Main page) is the key at which the sound plays at its own pitch: a bass note sampled at E1 has Root E1, so E1 on the keyboard plays it as recorded and E2 an octave up. Pressing the pad itself always plays the sound at its own pitch. With Root Auto-Detect ON (Additional Settings, page 2; ON to start; the Main page shows Auto-Detect: ON under Root Note), a sound loaded or recorded onto a pad of an S600 program gets its Root found for it, from the note in its name and the pitch heard in it, as Auto Map finds them (Chapter 10), with the fine tuning that puts it in tune. A sound with neither (a drum, noise) stays at C4. Set Root Note by hand any time.

Structure

An S600 program makes a sample into a playable instrument, laid out the way Akai’s S-series samplers did it. A sample feeds a filter; an amplitude envelope and a separate filter envelope shape it; an LFO adds vibrato and movement; velocity, pitch bend and modulation respond; it plays polyphonically or monophonically with portamento; and more than one sample can sound on each note.

Playing

  • Note On ON: the note holds while the pad or key is held, then releases. OFF: the envelope plays through on its own.
  • Mono with Legato: a note played while another is held changes pitch without restarting the envelopes, gliding if Portamento is set.
  • The wheels (Mod page): Bend Up/Down set the pitch bend range in semitones. The mod wheel brings in the LFO (at its Rate and Shape, after its Delay) as much as each amount says at full: Pitch is vibrato (100 swings two semitones each way), Level is tremolo, and Filter works by Filter Mode: LFO adds the LFO’s movement to the cutoff (two octaves each way at 100), OPEN opens the cutoff as the wheel goes up (three octaves at 100), a brightness control. They add to the LFO page’s own depths. An S600 MULTI instrument answers the same way.
  • Layer 2 sounds another pad’s sample on every note, through the same envelopes, filter and LFO, with its own level and tuning: layering, an idea taken from the S-series.

The S600 Waveforms

Four single-cycle oscillator waveforms come with the MPC 600 S: SINE, SAW, SQUARE and PULSE. Each is one cycle, 674 points at 44.1 kHz, which plays at C2. At power-on the MPC 600 S puts them on the card in MPC600S/s600 (any that are missing; ones you have changed are kept).

  1. Select a pad. On Save/Load set Mode LOAD and Type S600 OSC: it opens in MPC600S/s600. Choose a waveform and press <Load>.
  2. Turn its loop on: LOOP, then Loop ON, with Loop St at the start of the sound. A single cycle is only a few milliseconds long: looped, it plays as a held note.
  3. On an S600 program it then plays through the S600 filter, envelopes and LFO: a classic synth sound. Layer two (Layer page) for a thicker one.

Save your own single cycles with Type S600 OSC too, and they sit beside them.

The S600 LPF

The default filter on an S600 program is the S600 LPF, a four-pole low-pass modelled on the transistor ladder of the Minimoog Model D: 24 dB per octave, a resonant peak that grows steadily (about +14 dB at 80% resonance and +17 dB at 95%), self-oscillation at the top of the range, low end that holds up as resonance rises, and saturation that thickens and squashes loud signals. This is deliberately not an S-series filter: it is there because it sounds good on synth basses and leads. LPF to BPF 4 on an S600 program are the same filters as on an MPC program.

What an S600 MULTI Program Is

The MPC 600 S has three kinds of program, set by Type on Program Config:

A single sample stretched across the keyboard sounds less and less like itself the further it goes: an octave up, a piano note is thin and fast; an octave down, slow and dull. A multisample instrument avoids this by sampling the instrument at several notes (every few semitones) and, often, at two strengths, and playing each key from the nearest sample. The keygroups, zones and key tracking of S600 MULTI are taken from Akai’s S-series samplers (the S3000XL above all), streamlined: one instrument a program, two velocity zones a keygroup, and one set of envelopes, filter and LFO for the whole instrument.

An S600 MULTI program is still an S600 program: its Instrument has the S600 pages (Env1, Env2, Filter, LFO, Main), and those shape every note it plays. Its other pads are where its samples live; pressed, each plays its own sample at its own pitch, so you can hear it on its own.

How a Sample’s Note Is Found

From its name. A note name not inside a word, with an octave: Piano C3, pno_F#2, EP-Bb1, Bass E-1, AS C3 v2. Sharps as # (or s), flats as b; the last such note in the name counts. Numbers are not notes (Kick 01, C10), nor are letters inside words (Bass, Grand). The MPC 600 S counts middle C as C4 (note 60).

From its pitch. The pitch is measured on a stretch just after the attack (from 60 ms after it gets loud, up to a tenth of a second), with the YIN method: how alike the sound is to itself shifted by each possible period, the period where it is most alike giving the pitch, refined between samples. It finds pitches from A0 (27.5 Hz) to about C8 (4.2 kHz), in sounds with a steady pitch: notes of any instrument, synths, voices. Drums, noise and sounds with no clear pitch give none. The measured pitch also gives the Fine tuning that puts the sample in tune: a C4 sample 12 cents sharp gets Root C4, Fine −12.

From both (NAME+PITCH). Sample sets number their octaves differently: in some, middle C is C3. The name gives the note; the pitch heard decides its octave. A sample named C3 that is heard at middle C gets Root C4. With only one of them, that one is used.

Tips

  • Spacing: a sample every three or four semitones gives a natural instrument, each sample stretched at most a couple of semitones. One an octave apart gives a more stretched, lo-fi one, and is quick to record.
  • Play notes in any order: they are sorted by pitch at the end.
  • Length: long enough for the sound to ring out (pianos 3–5 s; plucks and basses 1–2 s). For a sound that sustains, record a shorter take and set a loop on each pad afterwards (Loop page).
  • Memory: a 3-second take at 29.76 kHz mono is about 360 KB in memory; 32 notes with two layers, about 23 MB.
  • Two layers want Normalize ALL, so the soft takes stay soft.

Sample Rate and Bit Depth

The rate and depth are the sound itself. A sound recorded at 26.04 kHz and 12 bits is stored as 26,040 twelve-bit samples a second. There is no full-quality copy underneath with an effect on top: what you hear is what was sampled. Nor is any particular sampler’s circuitry modelled. The character comes from the rate and the depth.

How a Take Is Made

  1. Capture. The MPC Sample’s converter digitises the input at 44.1 kHz, 16 bits. The OS holds the take as 32-bit floating point from here on, so nothing is lost between steps.
  2. Pitch (if set). The input is sped up by 2p/12 for p semitones (0 to +24) before it is sampled.
  3. Sampling at your rate. The new sound’s samples are taken at the exact moments a converter running at your chosen rate would take them. The input’s value at each of those moments, which falls between the 44.1 kHz samples, is reconstructed from the samples around it by band-limited interpolation (see the table below).
  4. Anti-Aliasing. With it ON, the interpolation’s cutoff is set to 45% of the new sample rate, so it is also a steep low-pass in front of the new rate. With it OFF, the cutoff stays at 45% of 44.1 kHz: everything the capture holds up to about 19 kHz is sampled, and whatever lies above half the new rate folds back down as aliasing, as it does in a sampler without an input filter.
  5. Normalize (if on). The take is scaled so its peak sits at the full scale of the new bit depth, as you would set the input gain on a sampler.
  6. Bit depth. Each sample is rounded to the nearest step of the depth: 2b steps across the full range, so 65,536 at 16 bits, 4,096 at 12, 256 at 8 and 16 at 4. The rounding is mid-tread (silence stays exactly zero) and the top is clipped at one step under full scale. There is no dither and no noise shaping. Quiet parts of a 12-bit or 8-bit sound carry the steps in them.

Playing Back

Every sound, at any sample rate and any pitch, plays back by zero-order hold (sample-and-hold), taken from the MPC60: each output sample is the stored sample at or before the play position, never blended with the next. For a sound below 44.1 kHz at its own pitch, the hold’s response is sin(πf/fs)/(πf/fs): −3.9 dB at the sound’s Nyquist frequency. All voices are mixed in 32-bit float. Pitching, below, explains it in full and compares it with the MPC3000 and with other methods.

Pitch

Pitch speeds the input up by 0 to 24 semitones before it is sampled, and the pad it lands on is tuned down by the same amount, so it plays at its own pitch. This is the old trick of sampling a record at 45 instead of 33 and tuning it down: more sound fits in the same memory, and the lower effective rate and the tuning down change its character. With Pitch at +12 and a rate of 26.04 kHz, the sound is effectively sampled at 13.02 kHz.

Pitching: How a Sound Plays at Another Pitch

A sound is stored as a list of numbers, one for each moment it was sampled. To play it at its own pitch, the MPC reads them one after another at the rate they were taken. To play it higher, it reads through them faster; lower, slower. Either way the play position soon falls between two stored samples, where there is no number. What a sampler plays at those positions is the biggest single part of how it sounds when pitched. There are two basic answers: take a stored sample as it is (hold), or work out a value between the samples from the ones around it (interpolate). This applies to every pad, S600 and S600 MULTI note, 16 Levels, Note Repeat, the arpeggiator and the sequence. Pitch on Sample New Sound, applied before a sound is stored, is a separate step, described at the end.

The MPC 600 S: Zero-Order Hold, Taken from the MPC60

The MPC 600 S pitches every sound the way the MPC60 does, taken from its sound chip (the L4003) as MAME’s reverse engineering of it describes:

  • The step. Each output sample, the play position moves on by the step: the sound’s rate over the output’s (44.1 kHz) times 2semitones/12, with Semi, Fine, the S600’s bend, LFO and glide in the semitones. The step is rounded to a 4096th of a sample (4.12 fixed point), as both the MPC60’s and the MPC3000’s chips hold it. Whole octaves are exact; between them the rounding is at most 0.11 cents an octave up, 0.21 cents at the sound’s own pitch, 0.42 cents an octave down and 0.85 cents two octaves down: far below what an ear can hear.
  • The output. Each output sample is the stored sample at or before the play position, held. It is never blended with the next.

What that sounds like:

  • Pitched down, some stored samples are output twice in a row and some once, in a pattern that repeats with the pitch. The waveform becomes a staircase, and the irregular pattern adds faint tones that are not in the sound: the metallic, stepped edge of late-1980s drum machines. The higher the sound’s highs, the more of this there is.
  • Pitched up, samples are skipped. Nothing filters the sound first, so highs pushed past half the output rate (22.05 kHz) fold back down as aliasing: the crunch of a pitched-up snare or hat.
  • At its own pitch, a 44.1 kHz sound plays exactly as stored. A sound below 44.1 kHz plays as a stepped waveform, each sample held until the next is due, as an early sampler’s converter output it.
  • Cost: one read per output sample, the least work any method takes, so pitching never limits how many notes can sound.

The MPC60 Itself

The MPC60 (1988) plays 12-bit samples at 40 kHz. Its L4003 chip steps through a sound in the same 4.12 fixed point (there relative to 40 kHz) and outputs the stored sample with no interpolation. MAME also models some processing of each sample (taken from code in the MPC3000 that converts MPC60 sounds, and presumed to model the chip’s own). The MPC 600 S takes the pitching only. It runs at 44.1 kHz, so its hold falls on a 44.1 kHz grid rather than 40 kHz; the pattern of repeated and skipped samples differs slightly, the character is the same. For more of the MPC60’s sound, also sample at 40 kHz and 12 bits.

The MPC3000

The MPC3000 (1994) uses the L6028 chip (also in the MPC2000 and MPC2000XL and the S2000, S3000 and S3200 samplers, per MAME). It runs at 44.1 kHz and steps in the same 4.12 fixed point, but it interpolates. Per MAME, each output is made from the eight stored samples around the position (three before it, the one at it, and four after), weighed by a windowed sinc: tap k at distance d from the position weighs sin(πd)/(πd) × (1 + cos(πd/4))/2, the weights of each of the 4096 positions between two samples normalised to sum to 1. (The S1000 before it used four points.) MAME’s authors note that these weights are the textbook ones and the chip’s own may differ slightly; one forum report describes the MPC3000 as interpolating linearly instead. The chip also has a resonant low-pass filter for each voice.

Pitched down, the MPC3000 is smooth: almost none of the stepped edge, and a slightly softer top. Pitched up, it still aliases, as the eight weights are not narrowed to filter the sound first, but less harshly than a hold. An eight-point interpolation was built and measured for the MPC 600 S during its development (see the table below); the MPC60’s hold was chosen for its character.

Common Modern Methods

  • Linear interpolation (2 points): a straight line between the two stored samples on either side. Cheap; pitched down it leaves a faint haze of images in the highs and dulls the top a little. The MPC 600 S used it for 44.1 kHz sounds up to LA Version 2.0G.
  • Cubic or Hermite interpolation (4 points): a smooth curve through four samples. Noticeably cleaner than linear for little more work; common in software samplers and trackers.
  • Windowed sinc, polyphase (8 to 64 or more points): the theoretically right reconstruction, cut short by a window. With more points the result is cleaner; computer audio software’s high-quality modes use long kernels. Done properly, the kernel’s cutoff is lowered when pitching up, so the sound is filtered before it is sped up and nothing aliases; some engines also oversample.
  • Pitch without speed (time-stretching, phase vocoder, granular methods) changes pitch while keeping the length. That is a different task: on the MPC 600 S it is Warp and the S-Series Timestretch. Pitching, here, changes the speed and the pitch together, like a turntable.

Pitch on Sample New Sound

Pitch on the recording pages (0 to +24, above) works before a sound is stored, and is band-limited. The take is sped up and sampled at the chosen rate in one step, by the windowed-sinc interpolation in Technical Specifications: Sampling (a Blackman-windowed sinc reaching at least 4/fc input samples each side). That is the same as playing a record faster into a sampler: every frequency moves up by the same ratio and nothing digital is added. With Anti-Aliasing ON, highs pushed above the new rate’s limit are removed, as by a sampler’s input filter; with it OFF they fold back as aliasing, as on samplers without one. The pad the sound lands on is then tuned down by the same amount, and from there on plays by the hold above.

Guitar and Bass: the INST L Input

A guitar or bass can plug straight into the MPC Sample. Plug it into the left input (on a stereo jack, a mono cable’s tip is the left side) and set Input to INST L, on Sample New Sound, Audiotrack Record or Input Effects.

INST L takes the left input alone, so the instrument is mono: it is sent to both sides, and anything sampled from it is saved as a mono sound, whatever the Mode field says. Before anything else (Input Effects, Input Thru and the recording all come after it), it goes through the instrument stage:

StageWhat it does

Why a Buffer Helps

A guitar amp’s input presents about 1 MΩ to the guitar (“Hi-Z”). A line input like the MPC Sample’s presents much less, and a passive pickup loads into it: its resonant peak drops and flattens, and the sound comes out duller and weaker. This happens in the analog circuit before the converter. Software cannot undo it; Bright gets part of the way back, and Gain makes up the level, but a hardware buffer fixes it completely:

  • A buffered pedal between guitar and MPC. Most Boss pedals (a DS-1, an OD-1, a TU-3 tuner and so on) have a buffer that works even when the pedal is switched off. Plug the guitar into the pedal and the pedal’s output into the MPC.
  • A dedicated buffer pedal, or a DI box with a high-impedance input: the same result.
  • Active pickups (and basses with an active preamp) already have a low-impedance output, so they plug straight in with nothing lost.

With a buffer or active pickups, turn Bright down to 0: nothing needs putting back. Keep Gain to taste. Into the Input Effects chain, the amp models (Bassman, AC30, JTM45, DC30, JC-120, B-15) and the Speaker Sim then make a complete guitar or bass rig.

Warp

SHIFT + Pad 15 opens Warp: the selected pad’s length and pitch changed independently. K1 Length (25–400%), K2 Pitch (±12), K3 Fine. Bars tells it how long the loop is, so it can show its BPM; B2 <Seq BPM> stretches it to the sequence’s tempo. Each change renders again in the background and plays; B3 <To Pad> puts it on the pad, keeping the original in memory. Only one warped sound is kept at a time.

Warp uses a phase vocoder with identity phase locking: each spectral peak keeps its own frequency and the frequencies around it keep their relation to it, which keeps sustained sounds clear rather than “phasey”. On a transient the phases start again from the original, so drums stay sharp; left and right move together, so the stereo image holds. Pitch is changed by stretching and resampling.

How the Two Modes Work

  • CYCLIC cuts the sound into cycles of a fixed length and repeats whole cycles (to make it longer) or drops them (to make it shorter), butted together. On a single-pitched sound with the cycle a whole number of its pitch periods, the joins line up and the result is clean; <AutoC> finds such a length by measuring the pitch. On anything else the joins show: that is the CYCLIC sound.
  • INTELL chooses its splice points: for each piece it searches nearby for the place where the sound best continues what the last piece was playing, and crossfades across the join. It suits speech, music and drum loops. Shorter pieces (high QUAL) follow the sound more closely; a wider crossfade (WIDTH) smooths the joins.

The parameters and modes are taken from the S1000’s TIME page. <Play> hears the result; <GO> puts it on the pad, keeping the original in memory.

Why slots: every insert runs all the time, live, on the MPC’s processor. The weights come from timing every effect, and the eight slots cap what the inserts can cost, whatever is chosen. Measured with everything at once (32 S600 voices with the ladder filter, all nine sends on heavy effects, all eight insert slots full), the inserts were the smallest part: about a seventh of what the voices take.

The Effects in Detail

Every effect has three controls, on K1–K3, each set from 0 to 127 like the knobs and shown in its own units. The effects run in stereo at 44.1 kHz. An effect named after a unit takes its character from it: how it clips, its EQ, how it moves.

In Print Effects they come in six categories, in this order: Reverb, EQ, Delay, Modulation, Drive/Dyn, Amp. In Input Effects any effect can go in any of the nine slots. In INPUT and PAD a chain holds at most one reverb and one delay.

As a send (TRACK/MIXER). Reverbs, delays and modulation add to the sound, so as sends only what they add comes back (the “wet” part). Drives, compressors, amps and EQs replace the sound, so as sends their whole output comes back, for parallel processing. A send of 0 is always silent and costs nothing.

USB Audio Out

Connected to a computer by USB, the MPC 600 S is also an audio input on the computer (a USB audio device, 44.1 kHz stereo; no driver). Choose it as the input in your recording software and record the MPC 600 S directly. What it sends is the full master mix: every pad and sequence, the track mixer with its EQ, sends and insert effects, Input Thru, and Master Effects, taken after Master Effects and before the main volume, so the recording does not change with the volume knob. The MPC 600 S follows the computer’s clock on its own. Switch it on with USB Audio Out on Additional Settings (OFF to start). With Input USB and Input Thru on, you hear the computer’s audio on the phones and line out, but it is never sent back to the computer, so there is no loop and the computer does not record its own audio twice (it is added after Master Effects, so Master Effects are not on it). The line, mic and instrument inputs go to the computer as usual.

Additional Settings

B1 <Settings> on MIDI Settings opens Additional Settings. It has two pages; B3 <Page 2> and <Page 1> switch between them. Page 1:

  • Button Lights: red (default), white, pink or off.
  • Pad Lights: light blue, red, white or off (default). Lit pads dim while pressed, more with pressure.
  • Pad Thresh: the lightest pad hit that counts, 1 to 60 (default 30; a setting saved at the old default of 12 is raised to 30). Raise it if pads play by themselves; lower it to play very softly. Pads played together all count, however hard each is played, and the data wheel and the buttons are never held back after a pad: a recording of the panel showed that pad hits do not move them, and that turning the wheel does not play pads. Turning a knob or moving the slider, or holding the wheel pushed in, presses on the panel: for 400 ms after a knob or the slider moves, and while the wheel is held down, pad hits lighter than velocity 80 are ignored, and so is a press of the wheel, B1–B3 or −/+ within 80 ms after a knob moves. On the mixer pages, for 300 ms after a knob or the slider moves, the wheel does not pan and a pad does not pick another channel. A knob, slider or volume reading that jumps far from the last is ignored unless the next reading confirms it, so a stray reading cannot jump a setting. The main volume is stricter: a jump far from where it is must hold for a tenth of a second, over at least three more readings that agree, so a burst of stray readings cannot drop the volume (a real turn, however quick, comes in small steps and is taken at once).

If Something Goes Wrong

  • A note will not stop: press STOP twice quickly to stop every sound.
  • The mixer does not answer MIDI CC: turn Mixer CC on and give each control a CC on the <CC> page.
  • The input cannot be heard: check Input Thru on Sample New Sound, and that Input Effects’ Input matches where you are plugged in.
  • Not sure what a page does: SHIFT + SAMPLE REC.
